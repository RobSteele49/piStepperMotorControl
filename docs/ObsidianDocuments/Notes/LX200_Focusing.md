Navigating the mechanical quirks of a classic Meade LX200 10" SCT is a bit of a rite of passage for amateur astronomers. Since you're mounted on a CGEM, you’ve already bypassed the old electronics issues, but the mirror mechanics remain the same.

Here is the breakdown of that focusing knob from your perspective (standing at the back of the tube, looking toward the sky).

Mirror Direction & Movement
On the LX200 Classic, the focus knob moves the primary mirror along a central baffle tube.

Counter-Clockwise (CCW): This moves the primary mirror Forward (toward the corrector plate/front of the scope). This pushes the focal point further back, which is helpful if you are using a long imaging train or a large 2" diagonal.

Clockwise (CW): This moves the primary mirror Rearward (toward the back of the scope). This pulls the focal point inward.

Reaching the Limits
Maximum Forward: Rotate the knob Counter-Clockwise until you feel gentle resistance.

Maximum Rearward: Rotate the knob Clockwise until you feel gentle resistance.

Pro-Tip: Never "torque" the knob when you hit the end of the travel. The LX200 uses a relatively simple threaded rod and pin system; forcing it can cause the mirror to bind or tilt unnecessarily.

Storage: Where to "Park" the Mirror?
For a telescope not in use, the general consensus among SCT experts is to store the mirror in the Rearward position.

Turn the knob Clockwise until the mirror is retracted toward the back of the cell.

Why? This keeps the primary mirror seated as low as possible on the baffle tube. It minimizes the risk of "mirror flop" during transport and reduces the amount of exposed, lubricated baffle tube that can collect dust or off-gas onto the optics over time.

A Note on Your CGEM Setup
Since you are using a German Equatorial Mount (GEM) instead of the original forks, your scope will undergo much more radical orientation changes (meridian flips).

The Golden Rule: Always finish your focus with a Counter-Clockwise turn. This pushes the mirror up against gravity. If you finish with a clockwise turn, gravity can cause the mirror to settle slightly later, ruining your focus or causing "shift" during a long exposure.
